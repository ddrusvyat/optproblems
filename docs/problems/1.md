## Problem Statement

The subgradient method is the workhorse of modern nonsmooth optimization. The algorithm applied to any Lipschitz continuous and
possibly nonconvex function $f\colon \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ proceeds according to the rule:
$$
x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta_t g_t,
\qquad
\textrm{where}\qquad g_t \in \partial f(x_t),
$$
Here $\partial f$ denotes the Clarke subdifferential [[1]](#ref-1) and $\eta_t$ are user-specified step-sizes often set as $\eta_t=\frac{c}{t^p}$ for some constant $c>0$ and exponent $p\in (0,1)$. Without any making any further assumptions, the subgradient method could in principle have wild behavior, for example forming limit cycles which are in no way critical for the problem [[2]](#ref-2). One way to remove such pathological situations from consideration is to assume that $f$ is a semi-algebraic function---meaning that its graph can be written as a union of finitely many sets, each cut out by finitely many polynomial inequalities. Semi-algebraic functions, and more generally those that are definable in an o-minimal structure, are nearly exhaustive in contemrary applications.


> **Conjecture:**  
> Suppose that $f$ is a semi-algebraic Lipschitz continuous function on $\mathbb{R}^d$. Then the subgradient method with a random Gaussian initialization $x_1\sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and stepsize of the form $\eta_t=ct^{-p}$ for some constnat $c\in \R$ and $p\in (0,1)$ generates a sequence of iterates $x_t$ whose limit points are all local minimizers of $f$, almost surely.








## § Known Partial Results
1. Smooth strict-saddle motivation

In the smooth setting, work of Lee et al. showed that gradient descent with random initialization can be expected to converge only to local minimizers, rather than to saddle points, under a favorable geometric picture [[3]](#ref-3). Although this result does not apply directly to nonsmooth objectives, it provided the main conceptual starting point for later work: non-minimizing critical points may be unstable and therefore unlikely to attract generic first-order methods.

2. Active strict saddles in the nonsmooth setting

Davis and Drusvyatskiy introduced the notion of an active strict saddle as a nonsmooth analogue of the strict-saddle idea from smooth optimization [[4]](#ref-4). Their work identifies a class of non-minimizing critical points that should behave unstably for subgradient-type methods, and it helped clarify how saddle-type behavior can still be described in a nonsmooth, stratified setting.

3. Convergence on tame functions

Davis, Drusvyatskiy, Kakade, and Lee proved that the stochastic subgradient method converges on a broad class of tame objectives, including semialgebraic locally Lipschitz functions [[5]](#ref-5). This established an important general convergence framework for nonsmooth nonconvex optimization and showed that limit points are stationary in a very broad setting. However, this result alone does not rule out convergence to stationary points that are not local minima.

4. Active manifolds, stratifications, and convergence to local minima

Davis, Drusvyatskiy, and Jiang pushed the theory further by studying how subgradient dynamics interact with active manifolds and stratified structure [[6]](#ref-6). At a high level, their work shows that this geometric structure can force convergence to local minimizers in generic definable settings. This is one of the strongest results in the direction of the present conjecture, since it moves beyond stationarity and toward actual local minimality.

5. Escape from active strict saddles

Bianchi, Hachem, and Schechtman showed that stochastic subgradient descent avoids active strict saddles under weak convexity together with additional geometric assumptions [[7]](#ref-7). In effect, this turns the geometric picture developed by Davis and Drusvyatskiy into an algorithmic avoidance result: once non-minimizing stationary points are known to have active strict-saddle structure, stochastic subgradient methods do not converge to them.
